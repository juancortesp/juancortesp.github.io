Bargain or rip-off? What a used car should really cost

I collected 71,220 AutoScout24 listings and trained a CatBoost model that estimates the fair price of a used car in Spain. I published it as a web calculator: enter the listing details and it tells you whether it's a bargain, fairly priced or expensive.

The question

Buying a used car without knowing if the price is fair

When you browse used-car listings, it's hard to tell whether a price is good. Every car differs in age, mileage, engine and trim, and eyeballing other listings is slow and misleading.

Which variables drive the price of a used car in Spain, and can we estimate a fair price accurately enough to flag cheap or overpriced listings?

Findings

What the data says

01

CatBoost clearly beats the rule-of-thumb approach

I compared three approaches on the same split (80% train, 20% test and 15% of train for validation), with imputation and encoding learned on train only to avoid data leakage.

  • The baseline mimics what a buyer would do: look at the median price of cars of the same make, model and age.
  • CatBoost handles categorical variables like make and model well, and cuts error by 33% vs the baseline.
  • On the test set, 14,244 listings it had never seen, the average error is €2,118 (8.5%).

02

Power is what matters most for price

Power is the model's most important variable, ahead of age and mileage: it sums up the car's class.

  • Make and model together account for almost 28%.
  • Depreciation is not linear: a car loses a lot of value in its first years, then the drop slows down.
  • Automatic gearboxes go with higher prices, partly because automatics tend to be newer and more powerful cars.

03

Diesel looks more expensive, but it isn't

In raw terms, diesel cars have higher prices than petrol cars. But it's a Simpson's paradox: diesel cars tend to be bigger.

Comparing the same model and age across 162 pairs, diesel comes out 1.6% cheaper (p = 0.024), and up to 5.3% cheaper at 9-10 years old.

The lesson: before drawing conclusions from an average, check whether the groups you're comparing are actually comparable.

04

A rule based on the model's real errors

To decide whether a listing is a bargain or expensive, I didn't make up a threshold. I looked at how the model's errors are distributed on the test set:

  • 80% of listings ask between −12% and +14% of the fair price.
  • Below that range: bargain; above it: expensive.
  • If it's a bargain, the app warns you: a very low price may hide an accident, faults or outstanding debts.

05

The province barely changes the price

Once you know the car, knowing where it's sold doesn't improve the prediction: province accounts for only 1.4% of the model.

To check this without bias, I compared each listing with the national median for the same model and age. Most provinces are within ±1%; the Canary Islands are 3% cheaper, and the +3% or +6% extremes come down to a handful of dealers.

Conclusions

What it means for car buyers

🎯

A fair price within 8.5%

Enough to separate clearly cheap or overpriced listings from normal ones, which is what a buyer needs.

⚙️

Look at power, age and mileage

They're the variables that move price the most. Province, hardly at all.

⛽

Don't trust raw averages

Diesel looks pricier, but for the same model and age it's slightly cheaper.

🔍

Always check a bargain

A price far below fair may be an opportunity… or hide something. The app warns you.

Limitations

  • These are asking prices, not sale prices. The final price is usually somewhat lower after negotiation.
  • It's a snapshot of October 2026 from a single portal, with that site's seller profile.
  • There's no seller type (dealer or private), so its effect couldn't be studied.
  • These are associations and predictions, not proven causation.

What I learned

  • Duplicates can inflate a model. The same car at several branches could end up in both train and test; removing it prevents overly optimistic results.
  • A good baseline gives context. "8.5% error" says little on its own; "33% less than eyeballing" says a lot.
  • A model is more useful when people can use it. Publishing it as an app, with a clear verdict and an explanation, makes it understandable to anyone.
